Supporting a loved one with hearing loss
When someone in your family has hearing loss, the way you speak and listen can make a huge difference to how included and relaxed they feel.
healthyhearing
+1

Small changes at home can turn exhausting conversations into enjoyable ones and help your loved one stay connected to the people and activities they care about.
widex
+1

Why your support matters
Hearing loss often appears gradually, so the person may not realise how much they are missing—or may feel worried about admitting it.
nhs
+1

Gentle, practical support from family is one of the biggest reasons people decide to get their hearing checked and make use of hearing aids or other help.
starkey
+1

Spotting the signs
You might notice that your loved one:
nhs
+1

Struggles to follow conversation, especially in groups or noisy places.
nhs
+1

Frequently asks you to repeat or thinks you are “mumbling”.
nhs
+1

Turns the TV or radio up loud compared with others.
nhs

Seems tired or stressed after social events because listening is hard work.
ncbi.nlm.nih
+1

If several of these sound familiar, it’s worth starting a supportive conversation.

Start the conversation gently
How you raise the topic matters.
multivu
+1

Choose a calm, private moment, not in the middle of a busy or emotional situation.
health.clevelandclinic
+1

Focus on care, not criticism: “I’ve noticed it’s harder to hear people in busy places—how is that feeling for you?”
widex
+1

Avoid phrases like “You never listen” or jokes at their expense, which can feel hurtful and make them shut down.
journals.lww
+1

Offer help: “Would you like me to come with you for a hearing check?” rather than “You must get hearing aids.”

Simple communication tips that really help
These easy steps are widely recommended by RNID, NHS teams and hearing charities.
england.nhs
+3

Get their attention first
Say their name or gently touch their arm before you start speaking.
rnid.org
+1

Don’t start talking from another room or with your back turned.
nwrc.ac
+1

Face them and let them see your mouth
Sit or stand where they can clearly see your face and lips.
rnid.org
+1

Make sure the lighting is good and avoid sitting with a bright light behind you.
rnid.org
+1

Don’t cover your mouth with your hands, hair or clothing, and avoid talking while eating or chewing.
hear4u
+1

Keep speech clear and calm
Speak at a normal volume and pace—shouting or over‑slowing can distort speech and feel aggressive.
rnid.org
+2

Use short, simple sentences and everyday words; avoid jargon and long, complicated explanations.
england.nhs
+1

If they don’t understand, say it in a different way rather than repeating the same words over and over.
rnid.org
+1

Cut down background noise
Turn down or pause TV, music or kitchen noise when talking.
rnid.org
+2

In cafés or restaurants, choose quieter corners or booths away from speakers and busy areas.
hear4u
+1

Check understanding and use writing when helpful
Pause occasionally to ask “Does that make sense?” or “Shall I repeat that?”.
rnid.org
+1

For dates, times, names or directions, write things down or text them—this removes stress for both of you.
rnid.org
+2

Making home more hearing‑friendly
Small changes around the house can make everyday life much easier.
england.nhs
+1

Switch on subtitles or captions for TV and streaming services.
nhs
+1

Seat your loved one where they can see everyone’s faces, especially at mealtimes or family gatherings.
hear4u
+1

In group conversations, encourage “one person speaking at a time” and repeat key points if needed.
healthyhearing
+1

Consider simple assistive devices such as amplified telephones, flashing or vibrating doorbells, and alerting systems for smoke alarms if recommended.
nhs
+1

Helping them get professional support
Your encouragement can make health services feel less intimidating.
widex
+1

Suggest a hearing check with the GP or local hearing service if hearing seems to be worsening or affecting everyday life.
nhs

Offer practical help: book the appointment together, arrange transport, and go with them if they’d find that reassuring.
multivu
+1

If hearing aids are fitted, support them while they get used to them—practice conversations in quiet settings and be patient while settings are adjusted.
nhs
+1

Help with simple maintenance like changing batteries or cleaning, using any written or easy‑read guides they are given.

Looking after feelings, not just ears
Hearing loss can affect confidence and mood, so emotional support is as important as practical tips.
journals.lww
+1

Acknowledge that it’s a big change and give space to talk about worries and frustrations.
widex
+1

Avoid saying “It doesn’t matter” when something is misheard—this can feel excluding and dismissive.

+1

Keep involving them in family plans and conversations; ask their preferences about venues, seating and noise levels.

+1

Try to keep patience and a sense of humour about inevitable mishearings without making them the butt of jokes.

Learn together and stay positive
The more the whole family understands hearing loss, the easier life becomes for everyone.
rnid.org
+1

Read reliable guides and leaflets together—for example, RNID’s communication tips and information on living with hearing loss.
rnid.org
+2

Agree simple “house rules” such as not talking from another room and turning down background noise for important chats.

Focus on what’s possible with the right support: better conversations, more confidence, and staying active in work, social life and hobbies.

Where to get more help (UK)
RNID (Royal National Institute for Deaf People) – offers information leaflets, communication tips and an Information Line for people with hearing loss and their families.
nhs
+1

NHS – your GP and local audiology service can arrange hearing tests, provide hearing aids, and link you to further support.

+1

Hearing charities and local groups – organisations such as Hearing Dogs for Deaf People and local hearing support groups offer practical advice and peer support.
